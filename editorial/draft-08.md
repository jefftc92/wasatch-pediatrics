### fever
Paragraph 1: Start with your child rather than the reading. Is your child drinking? Do they respond to you normally? Do they play a little once the fever eases, either on its own or after a dose of fever medicine? A child who does all three is usually well enough to watch at home. A fever means 100.4°F or higher taken rectally, in the ear or on the forehead. By mouth a fever is 100°F, and under the arm it is 99°F. Ear readings are not accurate under 6 months, so use a rectal or digital forehead reading at that age. A forehead reading counts only from a digital thermometer, because the paper strips are not accurate at any age. The height of the number matters less than how your child looks, and a reading of 103°F in a child who is drinking and playing is not an emergency.
Paragraph 2: At 104°F or higher, call us now, at any hour, even if your child seems well. Up to 106°F the heat itself is not damaging your child. We ask for the call because we would rather hear about a fever that high while it is happening than after it has passed. Above 106°F the heat does start to matter, so tell us that number when you call. Any fever in a baby under 3 months old means calling us now, at any hour, because at that age we check rather than wait. The threshold is 12 weeks, so count weeks rather than months if your baby is close to it. Do not give a baby under 3 months any fever medicine before we have seen them.
Paragraph 3: Call 911 if your child is not moving or is too weak to stand, even when the temperature itself looks ordinary. Call 911 if your child is hard to wake or cannot be woken at all. Call 911 if your child is struggling for each breath and can barely speak or cry. Purple or blood-colored spots or dots on the skin mean 911 as well. If none of those signs fit, an emergency room is the right place for a stiff neck or for a seizure during the fever. Take your child to an emergency room if they are awake but not alert, or act or talk confused. Take a baby under a year old to an emergency room if the soft spot on the head bulges or swells. The When To Call tab below lists the fevers that need a call within a day, and the Care Advice tab covers comfort and how much fever medicine to give.

### croup
Paragraph 1: Crying makes stridor louder, so comfort your child first and listen again once they have settled. If you can still hear stridor while your child is calm and resting, call 911, whatever your child's age. Call 911 if your child is struggling for each breath and can barely speak or cry. Call 911 if your child passes out or stops breathing. Blue lips or a blue face mean 911, whether or not your child is coughing at the time. Drooling, spitting or great trouble swallowing means 911. So does croup that started suddenly after a bee sting, a new medicine, or a food your child is allergic to.
Paragraph 2: If none of those signs fit, go straight to an emergency room when the ribs pull in with each breath, or when your child may have choked on a small object. A baby under a year old needs an emergency room now for stridor that comes only with crying or being upset. For a child a year old or older whose stridor comes only with crying, call us now, at any hour. Call us now as well if your child is working to breathe or breathing much faster than usual and can still speak or cry normally. Any fever in a baby under 12 weeks old means calling us now, at any hour. Do not give a baby that young fever medicine before we have seen them. The When To Call tab below lists the rest, including nonstop coughing and croup that keeps coming back.
Paragraph 3: Warm mist helps croup at every level, so start it while you wait for help or while you decide. Run a hot shower in a closed bathroom and sit with your child in the mist for twenty minutes, staying away from the water so that nobody gets burned. Then settle your child and listen again, and judge by what you hear after the mist rather than before it. A barking cough with no stridor is ordinary croup, and a child a year old or older with none of the signs above can stay at home. A baby under a year old with none of the signs above still needs a call within the day for a croupy cough. The Care Advice tab below has the rest of the home care.

### diaper-rash
Meta description: Diaper rash in babies: how to tell an ordinary rash from a yeast infection, when to start an anti-yeast cream, and which rashes need us to see your baby now.
Paragraph 1: Ordinary diaper rash comes from stool sitting against the skin, so wetness on its own is rarely the problem. Getting stool off the skin at every change is most of the treatment, and the Care Advice tab below gives the method. Cleaning alone clears most ordinary rashes, but not all of them. While your baby has diarrhea, a rash around the anus does better under a protective ointment such as Vaseline or Desitin, which keeps stool off the skin. A yeast rash looks different. It is bright red, and its border is a sharp line rather than a fade. The skin inside that border can look raw and weepy, and separate red spots or small pimples sit just beyond the border.
Paragraph 2: A bright red rash with a sharp border is a yeast rash, and it needs an anti-yeast cream today rather than more cleaning. Lotrimin AF is one such cream, and it is sold without a prescription. Start the same cream on an ordinary-looking rash that is no better after three days of cleaning with warm water and leaving the skin open to the air. Cleaning alone does not clear yeast. Some rashes need us rather than a cream. Call us now, at any hour, if the skin is bright red and peeling off in sheets, or if your baby has a fever and the redness is spreading. A baby under a month old needs to be seen now for water blisters or pimples, for yellow scabs, or for looking or acting abnormal in any way. The When To Call tab below covers everything else, including open sores and a rash that spreads beyond the diaper area.

### head-injury
Paragraph 1: Watch how your child wakes, talks, walks and keeps food down. Call 911 if your child had a seizure, was knocked out even briefly, or is hard to wake. Major bleeding you cannot stop is a 911 call too. If your child is not moving the neck normally, call 911 and keep the neck still. Confused talk, slurred speech, unsteady walking and weakness in an arm or leg mean 911 while they are happening, and an emergency room if they came after the injury and have already cleared.
Paragraph 2: An emergency room is also right for a child who is awake but not alert, unfocused or slow to respond, because those are the signs of a concussion. Go for neck pain after the injury, for vomiting two or more times, or for a severe headache or crying that will not stop. Go if your child cannot remember what happened or cannot store new memories, which a parent notices as the same question asked over and over. Blurred or double vision lasting more than five minutes also means an emergency room. So does an injury at high speed, such as a car crash, and so does a cut deep enough to need many stitches.
Paragraph 3: A big soft lump on the scalp looks frightening and usually means very little. The scalp has a large blood supply and bleeds easily into the loose tissue beneath, so a small injury can raise a large swelling. The skull bone separates that swelling from the brain. If your child is acting normally and has none of the signs above, the lump itself is the next thing to judge. Swelling wider than one inch means calling us now, at any hour. Feel the skull under the swelling as well, because a dent in the bone means calling us now too, whatever the lump looks like. Your child's age counts on its own here. A baby under a year old needs a call now, at any hour, after any head injury, even with no lump and none of the signs above. The When To Call tab below covers the rest.

### suicide-concerns
Lead: Call 911 now if your child has attempted suicide in the past 24 hours, or is confused, not making sense, or showing the effects of drugs or alcohol. Go to an emergency room tonight if your child attempted suicide within the past week but not in the past 24 hours. Go tonight as well if your child is threatening suicide, has a plan, or has needed a psychiatric hospital before for symptoms like these. If none of those 911 and emergency room signs fit your child and you are frightened, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, at any hour.
Paragraph 1: A plan means your child has a method in mind, such as taking drugs or using a weapon. A method that sounds vague or unlikely still counts as a plan. Take a threat or a plan as seriously as an attempt. You cannot judge from the outside whether your child means it, and the routes above do not ask you to. Stay with your child until someone else has taken over, and do not leave your child alone.
Paragraph 2: Making the house safer is the next thing to do, and firearms come first, because firearms cause most suicide deaths. Move any firearms out of the house to a relative or a friend for now. If a firearm stays in the house, unload it and lock it away. Then go through whatever else your child could reach, starting with medicines and alcohol. The Care Advice tab below has the rest of the home safety plan.
Paragraph 3: Ask your child directly whether they are thinking of killing themselves. Plain words are safer than careful ones, and asking the question does not put the idea in your child's head. Listen without arguing. Do not promise to keep what your child tells you secret, and say that you are getting help because you love them. Call us now, at any hour, if your child has thoughts of suicide, thoughts of seriously harming themselves, or themes of death in what they draw or write. You can call or text 988 instead, at any hour, whether or not your child is a patient here. For care that continues past tonight, our behavioral health services take children who are already patients at Wasatch Pediatrics. The When To Call tab below also has the option of asking us for suicide and depression resources rather than an appointment.

### constipation
Lead: A child who has had one painful stool will often hold the next stool back, and holding back is what turns a bad week into a pattern.
Paragraph 1: Your child is constipated if passing a stool hurts or brings on crying, if straining for more than ten minutes produces nothing, or if three or more days pass with no stool. Breastfed babies over a month old are the one exception. A breastfed baby that age can go four to seven days between stools without being constipated, as long as those stools stay soft and painless. A breastfed baby under a month old is the opposite case. That baby needs a call to us now, at any hour, because a newborn who rarely stools may not be getting enough breast milk.
Paragraph 2: A child who is not breastfed and goes four or five days without a stool almost always has pain and hard straining when the stool finally comes. That pain is the reason the holding back starts again the next day. Pain is one trigger. A power struggle is the other, and it is the most common cause of repeated constipation. The struggle usually starts over toilet training, and in a child who is already trained it often begins with the start of school, because some children refuse to use public toilets. Pressure and punishment make the holding back worse.
Paragraph 3: Go straight to an emergency room if your child's vomit is green, meaning the green of bile rather than the yellow of stomach juice. Call us now, at any hour, for belly or rectal pain that lasts more than an hour. Call now too for vomiting twice or more with a swollen belly, or for a new weak suck or new floppiness in a baby under a year old. The When To Call tab below has the rest. Otherwise constipation clears at home, with food and a calmer routine. If your child eats solid food, start tonight with pears, prunes or peas, and the Care Advice tab below covers the rest of the diet. Do not make your child sit on the toilet against their will, because forcing the sit starts the power struggle. Call us before you start a stool softener, so that we can go over which softener suits your child and what dose to use.
