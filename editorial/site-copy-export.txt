HOW THESE PAGES WORK, WHICH YOU NEED IN ORDER TO JUDGE THE WRITING
------------------------------------------------------------------
Each page is built from the fields below, in this order on screen:

  H1  ->  lead paragraph  ->  section heading  ->  body paragraphs
      ->  a live embedded panel from the American Academy of Pediatrics
      ->  a shared band with the practice contact routes

The AAP panel sits directly beneath the body copy and carries three tabs on the
same topic: Definition, When To Call, and Care Advice. The AAP writes and
updates it. Our copy is deliberately NOT supposed to repeat it. The division we
settled on:

  * Our prose carries every "Call 911 Now" and "Go to ER Now" sign in full,
    plus the judgement behind a decision, plus any threshold the judgement
    depends on (what counts as a fever, what counts as constipated).
  * Lower urgency tiers and home treatment belong to the panel. Our prose names
    the tab rather than repeating it.

The shared band below the panel already tells the reader how to reach the
practice, so a page that omits a phone number is not thereby faulty. It reads:

  Getting your child seen
  Talk to a nurse now, at any hour. Call your office main number, nights,
  weekends and holidays included. You will reach a nurse or a physician, not an
  answering service.
  Be seen today. Every office keeps same-day appointments for illness and injury.
  This evening or at the weekend. Most offices run After Hours Care, by
  appointment rather than walk-in.
  If you are worried, call.

THE AUDIENCE
------------
A parent with a sick or worried child. On a phone. Often at night. No medical
training, no patience, looking for one specific answer and scanning for it.

WHAT WOULD BE MOST USEFUL TO HEAR BACK
--------------------------------------
Sentences a demanding human editor would feel compelled to rewrite. Places the
writing is stiff, over-polished, riddling, or sounds assembled rather than said.
Places a frightened parent would have to read twice.

GROUP:            Fever and infection
PAGE TITLE (H1):  Fever in Children
META DESCRIPTION: What counts as a fever, how to judge your child rather than the number, which fevers mean 911 or an emergency room, and why any fever in a baby under 3 months means calling us at any hour.
SECTION HEADING:  Judging a fever by your child, not the thermometer

LEAD PARAGRAPH:
  Most childhood fevers come from ordinary viruses, and how your child behaves tells you more about this one than the thermometer does.

BODY PARAGRAPH 1:
  Start with your child rather than the reading. Is your child drinking? Do they respond to you normally? Do they play a little once the fever eases, either on its own or after a dose of fever medicine? A child who does all three is usually well enough to watch at home. A true fever means 100.4°F or higher taken rectally, in the ear or on the forehead, 100°F by mouth, or 99°F under the arm. Any of those readings counts, though an ear thermometer is not reliable under 6 months, and forehead strips are not reliable at any age. A high number is uncomfortable rather than dangerous, and a reading of 103°F in a child who is drinking and playing is still a fever to watch at home. At 104°F or higher, call us now, at any hour, even if your child seems well. The heat itself is still not damaging your child. We ask for the call because we would rather hear about a fever that high while it is happening than after it has passed.

BODY PARAGRAPH 2:
  Call 911 if your child is not moving or is too weak to stand, even when the temperature itself looks ordinary. Call 911 if your child is hard to wake or cannot be woken at all. Call 911 if your child is struggling for each breath and can barely speak or cry. Purple or blood-colored spots or dots on the skin mean 911 as well. If none of those signs fit, an emergency room is the right place for a stiff neck or for a seizure during the fever. Take your child to an emergency room if they are awake but not alert, or act or talk confused. Take a baby under a year old to an emergency room if the soft spot on the head bulges or swells. Any fever in a baby under 3 months old, which is 12 weeks, means calling us now, at any hour, because at that age we check rather than wait. Do not give a baby under 3 months any fever medicine before we have seen them. The When To Call tab below lists the fevers that need a call within a day, and the Care Advice tab covers comfort and dosing.

GROUP:            Coughs and breathing
PAGE TITLE (H1):  Cough in Children
META DESCRIPTION: Coughs in children often last three weeks and that is normal. What the different coughs mean, what to watch in your child's breathing, and when to bring them in.
SECTION HEADING:  The breathing matters more than the cough

LEAD PARAGRAPH:
  A dramatic cough frightens parents far more than the quiet, effortful breathing that actually worries us.

BODY PARAGRAPH 1:
  Watch your child's chest rather than listening to the cough. A loud, barking, hacking cough in a child who is playing, eating and talking in full sentences is doing no harm at all. The signs that change what we do are different ones. Watch for fast breathing, skin sucking in between or under the ribs, nostrils flaring with each breath, or a baby who cannot finish a feed without stopping for air. A child can show all four while barely coughing.

BODY PARAGRAPH 2:
  Call us straight away about any of those breathing signs, at whatever hour. Call as well about a cough that was clearly improving and then turned worse again, because that is the pattern that occasionally proves to be pneumonia. A cough still hanging on three weeks after a cold is ordinary, and it does not mean we missed anything.

PAGE 4: /symptom-checker/croup/
==============================================================================
GROUP:            Coughs and breathing
PAGE TITLE (H1):  Croup in Children
META DESCRIPTION: Croup's barking cough and noisy breathing: what stridor sounds like, what to do in the next twenty minutes, and which signs mean 911 or an emergency room.
SECTION HEADING:  Listen to the breathing in, not the cough

LEAD PARAGRAPH:
  Croup makes a barking, seal-like cough that gets worse at night. The cough is not what decides how urgent croup is. Listen instead for stridor, a harsh, raspy sound your child makes when breathing in.

BODY PARAGRAPH 1:
  Stridor is the sound to listen for, and crying makes it louder, so comfort your child first and listen again once they have settled. If you can still hear stridor while your child is calm and resting, call 911, whatever your child's age. Call 911 if your child is struggling for each breath, or if your child passes out or stops breathing. Lips or a face turning blue mean 911, whether or not your child is coughing at the time. Drooling, spitting or great trouble swallowing means 911. So does croup that started suddenly after a bee sting, a new medicine, or a food your child is allergic to.

BODY PARAGRAPH 2:
  If none of those signs fit, go straight to an emergency room when the ribs pull in with each breath, or when your child may have choked on a small object. A baby under a year old needs an emergency room now for stridor that comes only with crying or being upset. For a child over a year old whose stridor comes only with crying, call your office's main number now, at any hour. Warm mist helps croup at every level, so start it while you wait or while you decide. Run a hot shower in a closed bathroom and sit with your child in the mist for twenty minutes, staying away from the water so that nobody gets burned. The Care Advice tab below has the rest of the home care.

SECTION HEADING:  Which sore throats are worth a strep swab

LEAD PARAGRAPH:
  A sore throat alongside a runny nose and a cough is almost always viral. One that arrives on its own with a fever is worth testing.

BODY PARAGRAPH 1:
  Nobody can tell strep from a viral sore throat by looking, including us, which is why we swab rather than guess. Strep rarely causes a cough, so the combination that makes us want to test is a sore throat with a fever and no cough.   Book a swab rather than waiting it out. We take it in the office in minutes and give you the result before you leave. Treating strep shortens the illness and prevents the complications that follow an untreated infection. PAGE TITLE (H1):  Strep Throat in Children
META DESCRIPTION: Strep throat in children: how it differs from a viral sore throat, why a swab is the only way to know, and same-day testing at Wasatch Pediatrics.
SECTION HEADING:  Why the whole course of antibiotics matters

LEAD PARAGRAPH:
  Only a swab confirms strep, so book the test rather than guessing. Treating strep prevents complications that a viral sore throat never causes.

BODY PARAGRAPH 1:
  Strep is one of the few childhood throat infections where treatment changes the outcome rather than only the comfort. Your child should feel better within a day or two of starting the antibiotic. They stop being contagious after about 24 hours of it, which is the mark most schools use to decide when a child can return.

BODY PARAGRAPH 2:
  Finish the whole course even once your child feels well, because we treat strep to prevent the kidney and heart complications that follow a half-treated infection, not merely to settle the throat. Call us if the fever is still there after two full days of antibiotics, if a rash appears, or if your child cannot swallow their own saliva.

META DESCRIPTION: Ear pain in children: why it follows colds, what it looks like in a baby who cannot tell you, and how to get seen the same day.
SECTION HEADING:  Why an earache does not always mean antibiotics

LEAD PARAGRAPH:
  Ear pain usually arrives a few days after a cold, and in a baby it looks like pulling at the ear and a night nobody sleeps through.

BODY PARAGRAPH 1:
  Pain relief matters more than the antibiotic question in the first hours, because a child who is not hurting will drink and sleep, and both of those help them recover. We will often agree a plan of pain relief now and a call back in two days rather than a prescription today. Call us sooner if fluid drains from the ear, or if the bone behind the ear becomes swollen, red or tender.

PAGE 9: /symptom-checker/vomiting-and-diarrhea/
==============================================================================
GROUP:            Stomach and bowels
PAGE TITLE (H1):  Vomiting and Diarrhea in Children
META DESCRIPTION: Vomiting with diarrhea in children: how to keep fluids going, how long it usually lasts, and when a child needs to be seen.
SECTION HEADING:  Why oral rehydration solution beats juice and water

LEAD PARAGRAPH:
  Use oral rehydration solution rather than juice, sports drinks or soda, because the sugar in those makes diarrhea worse.

BODY PARAGRAPH 1:
  Not all fluids replace what a child is losing. Oral rehydration solution carries salt and sugar in the proportions the gut absorbs best. Plain water dilutes what little salt is left, and undiluted juice or soda pulls more water into the bowel and makes the diarrhea worse. The drink you choose therefore matters more here than the amount.

BODY PARAGRAPH 2:
  Call us if your child cannot keep fluids down at all, passes no urine for eight hours, or has gone quiet and floppy. We can give fluids in the office rather than sending you to an emergency room. Ask us as well before restricting food, since the bland-diet advice you may remember has gone: ordinary eating speeds the gut's recovery rather than slowing it.

GROUP:            Coughs and breathing
PAGE TITLE (H1):  Colds in Children
META DESCRIPTION: Colds in children: why so many are normal, what green mucus does and does not mean, and when a cold is worth a call.
SECTION HEADING:  The shape of a cold, and when it breaks

LEAD PARAGRAPH:
  Six to ten colds a year is normal for a small child, and green mucus is not a reason for antibiotics.

BODY PARAGRAPH 1:
  A cold has a shape, and knowing it saves most parents a phone call. Symptoms build for about three days, peak, then ease over the following week, and the cough outlasts everything else by two weeks. Green mucus arrives in the middle of that and means the cold is running its normal course, not that it has turned bacterial.

BODY PARAGRAPH 2:
  Call us when the shape breaks. A child who was recovering and then clearly went downhill is the pattern we want to hear about. So is a fever that arrives several days into a cold that had been settling. That turn is the one that occasionally proves to be an ear infection or a pneumonia. Call us about any cold in a baby under three months as well, whatever the shape.

PAGE 15: /symptom-checker/head-injury/
==============================================================================
GROUP:            Knocks, pain and injuries
PAGE TITLE (H1):  Head Injury in Children
META DESCRIPTION: Head injuries in children: which signs mean 911 or an emergency room, whether your child can sleep afterward, and when to call us.
SECTION HEADING:  The lump matters less than how your child acts

LEAD PARAGRAPH:
  Most head injuries damage only the scalp, and what your child does over the next few hours shows whether this one did more. Your child can sleep. Wake them once, two hours after they fall asleep, and check that they know you and can talk and walk normally.

BODY PARAGRAPH 1:
  Watch how your child wakes, talks, walks and keeps food down. Call 911 if your child had a seizure, was knocked out even briefly, or is hard to wake. Major bleeding you cannot stop is a 911 call too. If your child is not moving the neck normally, call 911 and keep the neck still. Confused talk, slurred speech, unsteady walking and weakness in an arm or leg mean 911 while your child has them. If any of those signs came after the injury and have already passed, drive to an emergency room instead.

BODY PARAGRAPH 2:
  An emergency room is also right for a child who is awake but not alert, unfocused or slow to respond, because those are the signs of a concussion. Go for neck pain after the injury, for vomiting two or more times, or for a severe headache or crying that will not stop. Go if your child cannot remember what happened or cannot store new memories, which a parent notices as the same question asked over and over. Blurred or double vision lasting more than five minutes also means an emergency room. So does an injury at high speed, such as a car crash, and so does a cut deep enough to need many stitches.

BODY PARAGRAPH 3:
  A big soft lump on the scalp looks frightening and usually means very little. The scalp has a large blood supply and bleeds easily into the loose tissue beneath, so a small injury can raise a large swelling. The skull bone separates that swelling from the brain. If your child is acting normally, the lump itself is the next thing to judge. Size still counts, and swelling wider than one inch means calling your office's main number now, at any hour. Feel the skull under the swelling as well, because a dent in the bone means calling your office's main number too, whatever the lump looks like. The When To Call tab below covers the rest.

  A rash that stays visible when you press a glass against it needs emergency care now. Almost every other rash can wait until morning.

META DESCRIPTION: Hives in children: why they travel, what usually triggers them, what helps the itch, and the swelling that means an emergency.
SECTION HEADING:  Hives alone, and hives that mean anaphylaxis

LEAD PARAGRAPH:
  Hives with swelling of the lips or face, or any trouble breathing, mean 911. Hives on their own usually mean an antihistamine.

GROUP:            Stomach and bowels
PAGE TITLE (H1):  Constipation in Children
META DESCRIPTION: Constipation in children: what actually counts as constipated, why a child starts holding back, and when to call us.
SECTION HEADING:  Why children hold stools back, and why that keeps constipation going

LEAD PARAGRAPH:
  A child who has had one painful stool will often hold the next one back, and holding back is what turns a bad week into a pattern.

BODY PARAGRAPH 1:
  Your child is constipated if passing a stool hurts or brings on crying, if straining for more than ten minutes produces nothing, or if three or more days pass with no stool. Breastfed babies are the exception. A breastfed baby over a month old can go four to seven days between stools without being constipated, as long as those stools stay soft and painless. A breastfed baby under a month old is different, and needs a call to us now, at any hour, because a newborn who rarely stools may not be getting enough breast milk.

BODY PARAGRAPH 2:
  Any child who is not breastfed and goes four or five days almost always has pain and hard straining when the stool finally comes, and that pain is the reason to hold on again. Pain is one trigger. A power struggle is the other, and it is the most common cause of repeated constipation. The struggle usually starts over toilet training, and in a child who is already trained it often begins with the start of school, because some children refuse to use public toilets. Pressure and punishment make the holding back worse.

BODY PARAGRAPH 3:
  Go straight to an emergency room if your child's vomit is green, meaning the green of bile rather than the yellow of stomach juice. Call us now, at any hour, for belly or rectal pain that lasts more than an hour. Call now too for vomiting twice or more with a swollen belly, or for a new weak suck in a baby under a year old. The When To Call tab below has the rest. Otherwise constipation starts at home, with changes to food and a calmer routine, and the Care Advice tab below covers them. Call us before you start a stool softener, so that we can go over which one suits your child and how much to give.

LEAD PARAGRAPH:
